# Resolving a definition's references

A checklist for step 3. Some definitions do not carry their own meaning: they send the reader to another clause, a schedule, an exhibit or an outside agreement. Each of those pointers is checked here, using only what is in the document you were given.

## The decisions

1. **Resolved** — the supplied document contains the target, and the target can carry the meaning the definition sends to it.
2. **Broken** — the target is meant to be inside this document, and this document cannot resolve it. This is a finding.
3. **Outside the supplied scope** — the target is an outside agreement, or a companion document, schedule or exhibit that was not supplied. Informational, never a broken internal reference. Name the document.
4. **Ambiguous** — the wording could point at more than one target and the document does not settle it.
5. **Not enough evidence** — the material in front of you cannot support any of the above.

## Rules

Cite the smallest sufficient evidence. Quote the wording that resolves the pointer, and its unit; do not list every place the words happen to appear.

An incidental mention in prose does not resolve a heading, a section, a schedule, an exhibit or an incorporated definition. A clause that says a term "has the meaning given in Schedule 3" is resolved only where Schedule 3 is supplied and gives it. If Schedule 3 is not in the document you were given, that is decision 3, and the lawyer should be told which schedule is missing.

Prefer operative wording over unexplained headings, titles and fragments of proper names.

Keep the wording you relied on separate from the exact source span the finding is reported against: the first is why you decided, the second is what the lawyer will be looking at.

An unresolved queue fails closed. Where a definition's pointers could not all be checked, the broken-reference finding is not reported at all, and the review page says which pointers were left unchecked.
